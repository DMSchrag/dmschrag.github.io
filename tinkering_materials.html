What is usefull what is not

What are some good properties

For once when I started tinkering more it hit me like a truck that stuff costs money, especially if you want quality. I would really like to use Maxon DC Motors but they are just way more expensive than what I could get on Aliexpress. The availability of Datasheets and Instructions are also needed if you go more into electronics hardware, I can't probably use a nice microcontroller if there is absolutely no documentation about it. It hasn't happened to me yet but I have already purchased some small knock off microcontrollers which were really difficult to set up since documentation was lacking. My parts when I build something needs to be interoperable, when I tinkered on my commuter bike really wanting to mount that basket on my bike, there was no other choice then to design and build a mount on my own. I could have bought a nice bike basket but that wouldnt have fit with my expectations. Also a cheap basket and 3D printing a bit of plastic is quite cheaper than buying one off the shelve. In the table you can see some of the materials used in class, and how tinkerable I personally evaluated them.

One time I bought another cheap microcontroller because it looked almost the same as the normal one. I thought it would work the same and save me money. Then I spent more time searching for drivers and trying different settings than actually using it. After that I started looking more at documentation before buying something.

I also wanted to look at materials that were not only used in class. I added some recycled materials, flexible materials and even materials which are grown instead of produced normally. Some of them sound really good at first but they also have their own problems.

Material Table

I rated the materials depending on what I would actually use them for. A material can be very good for one project and quite bad for another one. I also looked at what happens to the material after I am finished with it, because throwing away every prototype is not really a good solution either.

Material Price and Availability Tools and Modification Compatibility Repair and Reuse Safety Sustainability Critical Problems My Ranking
Cardboard Very cheap and easy to find. I can normally use old packaging instead of buying it. Very easy to cut, fold, tape and glue. I only need simple tools. Works with tape, glue, string, wood, plastic and electronic parts. Easy to repair with more tape. It can also be replaced without losing much money. Mostly safe, but knives, hot glue and fire can still be a problem. Reusing packaging is good because the material already exists. Clean cardboard can normally be recycled. Tape, plastic coating and lots of glue can make it difficult to recycle. It is also weak against water. Very high for first prototypes. I would use it to test the size and shape before making the real part.
Reclaimed wood Can be very cheap or even free, but I cannot always choose the exact size or type. Easy to saw, drill, sand and screw together with normal woodworking tools. Works well with screws, brackets, glue, hinges and 3D printed connectors. Easy to repair and reuse. A damaged section can often just be cut away or reinforced. Splinters, dust, old nails and unknown paint can be a problem. It is better to reuse existing wood than to buy new wood every time. I might not know which paint, glue or chemical was used on it before. Very high for frames and structures. I would use it when the exact appearance is not so important.
Plywood Not too expensive and easy to find in many different thicknesses. Easy to saw, drill, sand, mill and laser cut. Works with screws, glue, brackets, hinges and press fit connections. It can be reused when I use screws. Glued parts are much harder to separate. Wood dust and laser cutting smoke need to be taken seriously. Wood can grow again, but plywood also contains glue between every layer. The wood might come from badly managed forests and the glue can make recycling difficult. High for stronger prototypes. It is useful when cardboard is too weak but metal would be too much.
Cork sheet Not too expensive and quite easy to find in craft and hardware shops. Easy to cut, shape and glue without heavy tools. Works well as padding, a grip or something between two harder parts. Small pieces can be reused, but torn edges are difficult to repair properly. Mostly safe. Cutting and sanding can still create dust. Cork can be taken from the tree without cutting the whole tree down. Some cork sheets contain a lot of glue or have a plastic backing. High for padding and grips. I would use it for parts which should feel soft or reduce vibration.
Bamboo plywood More expensive and harder to find than normal plywood. It can be cut, drilled and sanded like wood, but it can be quite hard. Works with normal screws, brackets and wood connections. Strong and reusable, but damaged layers can be difficult to repair. Dust and the glue inside the material need to be considered. Bamboo grows quickly, which sounds good, but it still needs to be processed and glued together. It is often transported a long distance. I also do not always know how it was grown or how workers were treated. Medium to high for visible parts. I would use it when I want something strong which also looks nice.
Recycled PET felt More expensive than normal fabric but it is becoming easier to find. Easy to cut with scissors or a knife. Thick sheets can be harder to cut cleanly. Works with sewing, clips, glue and hook and loop fasteners. It can be reused when it is clipped or sewn. Glue makes it more permanent. Mostly easy to handle, but cutting can release small fibres. It gives old plastic another use instead of using only new plastic. It is still plastic and can be difficult to recycle again, especially when it is mixed with glue. High for soft covers and padding. I would use it for enclosures, bags and sound absorbing parts.
PLA filament Fairly cheap and very easy to find. Easy to change in a 3D model and print again. It can also be drilled, sanded and glued. Very useful for custom mounts, cases, adapters and connectors. I can print another part when it fails, but the broken part is still waste. The printer gets hot and should not be left without attention. Ventilation is also useful. PLA can be made from plants, but this does not mean that it disappears when I throw it away. Most normal recycling systems do not take printed PLA. Failed prints and support material can create a lot of waste. Very high for custom shapes. It worked well for my bike basket mount because I could change the model and print another version.
Recycled PETG filament More expensive than normal PLA and not available everywhere. It can be printed on many normal printers, but the settings need more attention. Very useful for stronger mounts, covers and outdoor parts. Parts can last longer than PLA parts, but broken prints are difficult to repair. Printer heat, fumes and unknown additives should still be considered. It uses recycled plastic instead of only new plastic. Printing still uses energy. Different additives and colours can also make recycling harder later. Very high for functional printed parts. I would choose it when PLA might be too weak or too sensitive to heat.
TPU filament More expensive than PLA but still easy to buy. The design is easy to change, but the material can be annoying to print. Useful for grips, flexible joints, straps, covers and vibration reduction. Flexible parts can last a long time, but tears are difficult to repair. The same printer safety problems apply as with other filaments. A durable part does not need to be replaced often, which is good. TPU is difficult to recycle and failed prints are normally just plastic waste. High for flexible parts. I would only use it when I actually need movement or flexibility.
Aluminium extrusion More expensive than wood, but easy to find in standard sizes. Easy to cut and assemble when I have the correct tools and connectors. Very compatible with brackets, wheels, hinges, panels and other standard parts. Very easy to take apart and use again for another project. Sharp edges, metal chips and heavy structures can cause injuries. New aluminium needs a lot of energy, but it can be recycled and used for a very long time. Mining aluminium can damage land and use a lot of energy. The working conditions and energy source also matter. Very high for modular frames. I would use it for a project which I know I will rebuild several times.
Silicone rubber More expensive and normally bought from a specialist supplier. I need to measure and mix it properly. Making the mould also takes time. Useful for seals, grips, feet, moulds and waterproof parts. It can last a long time, but damaged silicone is difficult to repair. Gloves and ventilation are important before the silicone has fully cured. A long lasting part can reduce replacement. Cured silicone is difficult to recycle. Mixing mistakes can also waste a full batch. Medium to high for waterproof parts. I would use it when normal plastic or fabric cannot make a proper seal.
Mycelium composite Growing kits can be affordable, but ready made products can be expensive. It needs time, the correct growing conditions and a clean working area. It can be grown inside a mould and shaped after drying. Works for lightweight shapes, packaging and insulation, but not so well with screws or heavy loads. It can be damaged by water and strong impacts. The result can also be different every time. Contamination, spores, dust and fire behaviour need attention. It can use agricultural waste and may be easier to dispose of than plastic foam. Coatings, drying energy and failed growth can reduce the advantage. It is not automatically sustainable just because it is grown. Medium for experimental shapes. I think it is interesting to work with, but I would not trust it yet for an important structure.
Conductive fabric Quite expensive compared with normal fabric and normally bought online. I need information about resistance, washing and how to connect it properly. Can be cut, folded, sewn and connected with snaps or conductive thread. Useful for wearable sensors, switches and flexible circuits. Connections can be repaired, but the conductive coating can wear away. It should be used with low voltage. Short circuits, batteries and heat still need attention. The metal and textile are mixed together, which makes recycling difficult. I normally do not know much about where the metal came from or under which conditions the fabric was produced. High for wearable experiments. I would use it when normal wires would make the project too hard or uncomfortable.

What I noticed from the table

There is not really one material which is the best. Cardboard is probably the best when I only want to test the size of something. It would be a terrible choice for my bike basket mount because the mount needs to deal with rain, vibration and weight. For that I would rather use PETG, aluminium or maybe a strong piece of wood.

Being able to take something apart is also more important than I first thought. Screws, brackets, stitching and clips let me change my mind. Glue can be fast, but after using enough glue I sometimes end up with two materials which cannot be separated or reused anymore.

Sustainability is also not as simple as choosing something natural or recycled. Bamboo grows quickly, but it can still travel a long distance and contain a lot of glue. Aluminium takes a lot of energy to make, but a frame can be taken apart and used again for many projects. PLA comes from plants, but I still cannot put my failed prints into normal recycling.

I think the best thing I can do is first use as little material as possible, use material which is already there, and build things in a way that lets me take them apart again. After that I can look at which new material actually makes sense for the project.

Documentation also stays important. A material or component can be cheap, but if I do not know how strong it is, what temperature it can handle, or how to work with it safely, then it is not really as useful as it first looked.

Conclusion

For me a tinkerable material needs to be affordable, understandable and easy to change. It should also work with the tools and parts I already have. I do not want to be afraid of making a mistake because the material was too expensive or impossible to replace.

At the same time I also need to think about where the material comes from and what happens to it afterwards. A cheap material is not really cheap when I need to throw it away after every small mistake. The best material is the one which lets me test something, fix it, learn from it and hopefully use most of it again.
